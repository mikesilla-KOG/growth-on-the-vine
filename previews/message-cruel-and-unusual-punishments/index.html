What did the Supreme Court say about cruel and unusual punishment in 1958?

▶ Watch this part (0:00)

Short answer: He quotes that the Eighth Amendment must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency of a maturing society, and tells of a half-day deserter who lost citizenship—the Court called that cruel and unusual for that crime.

🎙From the sermonTranscript of this part · Pastor David G. Kincer

▶ 0:00 Today we’re going to look at the issue of the Eighth Amendment of the Constitution: what is cruel and unusual punishment? What is cruel and unusual punishment that the Constitution says we cannot punish people with? The Supreme Court in 1958 made this statement: the Eighth Amendment must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society. A soldier had deserted for half a day, came back, turned himself in; he was sent to the brig, he was discharged with an undesirable discharge, and then they took his citizenship away. And the Supreme Court said no—the Eighth Amendment—that is cruelty and that is an unusual severe punishment for that crime. So remember they said this statement: the Eighth Amendment must draw its meaning from the evolving standards

▶ Watch this part (11:52)

Short answer: From Deuteronomy 19:19 he says punishment should be publicized to deter. Cruel and unusual, to him, is authorities not punishing and publicizing, not informing citizens of destructive results, and missing morals and self-control—the puzzle piece—not decency.

🎙From the sermonTranscript of this part · Pastor David G. Kincer

▶ 11:52 So penalizing is for deterring crime. Deuteronomy 19:19: put evil away from your midst, and those that remain shall hear and fear and shall from henceforth commit no more such evil. Punishment is to be publicized, to be public, to cause those that are coming up behind to stay away from a life of crime.

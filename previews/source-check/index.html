Outside source check — a third label

When a sermon states a statistic or a historical or factual claim, and we have checked it against outside sources, this teal callout shows what the source says (with the source’s name and a link) next to the pastor’s untouched words. Nothing here is live.

One real example

From the live page Division Part 1 (section “Why do young people leave the church?”). On wide screens the pastor’s words (left) and the source check (right) sit side by side; on a phone they stack, sermon first.

🎙From the sermonDivision Part 1 · Pastor David G. Kincer▶ 0:40

Number one, the statistic that 75% of young people are out of the church when they leave high school and go into college has always been there since I’ve been a Christian. In fact, it was a little higher. If it’s only 75%, we gained some ground. It used to be 80-some percent. Okay, that’s not a new statistic.

🔎Outside source check◐Partly confirmed

In short: the direction holds up — most young people who attended church as teens do step away from regular attendance in young adulthood. The exact 75% is not what the major studies found, and we found no study behind an earlier “80-some percent.”

What the sources say
  • ✔ConfirmedA large majority leave regular church attendance after high school.

    Lifeway Research (2007): of adults 23–30 who had attended a Protestant church regularly for at least a year in high school, 70% stopped attending regularly for at least a year between ages 18 and 22. Barna Group (2011): 59% of 18–29-year-olds with a Christian background disconnected from church after age 15, “permanently or for an extended period of time.” Barna (2019): 64%.

  • ≠Source differs“75%.”

    None of these studies reports 75%. The closest are 70% (Lifeway), 64% (Barna 2019) and 59% (Barna 2011). They also count something a little different from “out of the church”: Lifeway counts stopping regular attendance for at least a year, and says about two-thirds of those dropouts later returned at some level (35% attend twice a month or more, another 30% attend more sporadically).

  • ✔Confirmed“That’s not a new statistic.”

    Lifeway published its 70% figure in 2007, and Lifeway’s own release says “lots of alarming numbers have been tossed around regarding church dropouts.” A 2008 seminary dissertation set out to test a circulating claim that “70 to 90%” of youth-ministry graduates leave. Both come before the 2011 Barna book.

  • ?Not confirmed“It used to be 80-some percent.”

    We found no survey that measured an 80-some percent rate. Higher figures (70–90%) were being quoted in the 2000s, but the 2008 dissertation treats them as a claim to be tested, not a measured result. That dissertation, a study of twelve Southern Baptist churches, found an 88% retention rate and that over 70% of respondents never dropped out between 18 and 22 — a limited sample, not a national count. “Not confirmed” means we could not find a source either way; it does not mean he is wrong.

In this passage he does not say where the 75% or the “80-some percent” comes from. Read the full section and hear it at 0:40. He calls the book “You Lost Us” in the transcript; Barna’s book is titled You Lost Me. The transcript is left exactly as spoken.

Sources
  • Lifeway Research, “Reasons 18- to 22-Year-Olds Drop Out of Church” (Aug 7, 2007); data report: Church Dropouts: How Many Leave Church and Why (PDF). Survey of 1,023 adults 18–30, April–May 2007.
  • Barna Group, “Six Reasons Young Christians Leave Church” (Sep 27, 2011); 1,296 interviews with 18–29-year-olds.
  • Barna Group, “Church Dropouts Have Risen to 64%—But What About Those Who Stay?” (Sep 4, 2019).
  • J. Brandon Shields, “An assessment of dropout rates of former youth ministry participants in conservative Southern Baptist megachurches”, Southern Baptist Theological Seminary dissertation (2008), abstract.

Checked October 4, 2026, against the public pages above. These are U.S. surveys about people who attended church as teens. This check reports what the studies found; it does not judge the sermon’s message.

The four states (layout samples)

Layout samples only — the wording below is placeholder text, not a real check. Every state is shown as a symbol plus a word.

🔎Outside source check✔Confirmed

Sample. The source reports the same fact or number the sermon gave (within normal rounding). Say what the source says, with its name, date and link.

🔎Outside source check◐Partly confirmed

Sample. Some parts of the claim check out and others don’t, or the source supports the general point but not the exact figure. List each part with its own state.

🔎Outside source check?Not confirmed

Sample. We looked and could not find a reliable source either way. This is not the same as “false.” Say what we searched.

🔎Outside source check≠Source differs

Sample. A reliable source gives a different figure or fact than the sermon. Show both, plainly, without softening either.

Rules (also written into the two workflow skills)

  • Use it only for factual or statistical claims (numbers, dates, historical or scientific facts). Never for doctrine, interpretation or opinion.
  • Scripture stays blue and the pastor’s words stay amber, exactly as they are. His words are never softened, trimmed to fit, or reworded to make a check come out nicer.
  • Report what the source says, honestly, in either direction. Use primary sources where possible (the study, agency or archive itself). Always give the source name, date and link.
  • Check each part of a claim separately. Mixed results get the overall state “Partly confirmed.”
  • “Not confirmed” means we found nothing either way — never say or imply he is wrong without a source that says so.
  • Place the check directly beside or just after the sermon block it checks, never inside Scripture or sermon text.

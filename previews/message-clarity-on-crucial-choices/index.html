What three crucial choices does he set out?

▶ Watch this part (0:35)

Short answer: From Matthew 13:30 and Malachi 3:18 he names choosing to be good (best), wicked (worst), or trying to do good without God as humanists do—or a Christian who believes but does not serve God. Being a humanist does not necessarily mean wicked, nor a Christian necessarily not wicked.

🎙From the sermonTranscript of this part · Pastor David G. Kincer

▶ 0:35 Today we’re going to look at clarity on crucial choices—whether we make bad choices, we can make better choices, and we can make the best choices. We’re going to be dealing with three choices this morning. In Matthew 13:30, let both the good and the wicked grow together until the harvest at the end of the world. In Malachi 3:18, you shall again discern between the righteous and the wicked—first choice, second choice—but then he’s adding another choice between one who serves God, which would be one that does good and right, and one who doesn’t, which could be a humanist that doesn’t believe in God and the Bible, or a Christian that believes in God but does not serve God. So we got these three choices: to choose to be good, which is the best choice; to be wicked, which is the bad choice, the worse; and a choice to try to do good without God, which the humanists try to do. Now being a humanist doesn’t necessarily mean you are wicked, and being a Christian does not necessarily mean you’re not wicked.

Where do humanists and Bible followers agree on “good”?

▶ Watch this part (3:17)

Short answer: He cites humanist billboards “good without God” and Buffett/Gates charity, and Galatians 6:10 do good to all. The Manifesto’s “short of harming others” means both camps must punish, correct, or remove evil so others are not hurt—or else their ideology is wicked.

🎙From the sermonTranscript of this part · Pastor David G. Kincer

▶ 3:17 Now the Bible said that the wicked and the righteous and the one that don’t serve God are supposed to grow together. So how do we do this? Number one, the humanist and the Bible followers agree on good. The humanists say that Warren Buffett and Bill Gates are good without God because they give billions to charity. They also have billboards going all throughout the country: Are you good without God? Millions are. They have another one that says millions of people are good without God—you don’t need religion to be a good moral person. And so humanists say they’re out for good. They believe in good. They believe we should do good. And the Bible says in Galatians 6:10, as we have opportunity let us do good to all people. And so we are in agreement that we’re supposed to do good. So good is the basics for living together and growing together. In the Humanist Manifesto it says, short of harming others, individuals should be permitted to express and pursue their desired lifestyles. The key here is short of harming others. So we must conclude that humanists and Bible followers must deal with bad people—whether they’re bad humanists or bad Bible followers. To get along we must agree that evil must be punished, corrected, or removed from society so they can’t hurt others. And also if humanist or Bible followers do not agree to do good and punish criminals, they are a wicked ideology, a wicked system of faith, a wicked philosophy, because they’re not agreeing to do good.

Short answer: First, neither humanists nor God-followers may lie or harm others physically, financially, mentally, or spiritually. Second, when either hurts others they must be punished or corrected as criminals, and if they will not change, separated from society.

🎙From the sermonTranscript of this part · Pastor David G. Kincer

▶ 11:10 Now how can God’s people and humanists and the wicked grow or increase together? We’re going to summarize it. How can this possibly work out? Okay, we’ve given you the steps. We’re going to sum those steps. First of all, humanist nor God followers may lie to other people or do harm to other people physically, financially, mentally, or spiritually if we’re going to grow together and work together as a community, as a nation. And when we do we need to be punished or corrected for the wicked behavior, as we are no longer just believers or non-believers but we are criminals—and criminals need to be corrected, punished, and if we don’t change our harmful ways we need to be separated from society.

What three choices does he sum up at the end?

▶ Watch this part (12:50)

Short answer: Remember God cannot lie, God is love, love does no harm (Romans 13:10); laws should protect citizens from harm. Manifesto agrees “short of harming others.” Three choices: follow Jesus and don’t harm; reject Jesus and don’t harm (humanist); or follow/reject but hurt others—criminal wickedness requiring punishment. We must do good, not only talk.

🎙From the sermonTranscript of this part · Pastor David G. Kincer

▶ 12:50 Now we’ve got to remember and recall that God cannot lie, that God is love, and love does no harm to a neighbor. And in Romans 13:10 it says love fulfills the law. Every law written should be written to protect its citizens from harm, both from government and other citizens—and that’s the kind of laws that work. We got to remember these things, these high priorities. Next is we got to remember in the Humanist Manifesto they said, short of harming others, individuals should be permitted to express their likings and pursue their lifestyles as they desire. But the key is short of harming others. They agree with the Bible that they believe is a myth—we don’t have a right to hurt others. So we can unite around good, around not hurting others, and live amongst each other though one of us believes in God and the other doesn’t. So summing it up, there’s three choices: follow Jesus, the Bible, and don’t harm others; reject Jesus, the Bible, and don’t harm others—that would be the humanist; and number three, follow or reject Jesus in the Bible but we hurt others by criminal wicked behavior requiring punishment. In summation, we cannot just talk about doing good—we must do good.
